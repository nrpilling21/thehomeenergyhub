Is it cheaper to leave heating on all day? For a home with a gas boiler, no. Leaving the heating on low all day uses more gas than heating the house only when you need it, because a warm house loses heat faster than a cool one, all day long. The idea that it costs more to "warm the house back up" than to keep it warm is one of the most persistent energy myths in the UK, and the Energy Saving Trust and MoneySavingExpert both class it as one.

**Quick answer (gas at 7.97p per kWh, October to December 2026 price cap):**

- **Heating a typical 3-bed semi to 20°C for 24 hours on a 5°C day:** about £6.48 of gas.
- **Heating the same house to 20°C only when you are home (8 hours a day):** about £5.75. You save about 73p a day, roughly £110 over a winter.
- **Turning the thermostat down from 20°C to 18°C, all day:** saves about the same, roughly 13%.
- **When "on low" does make sense:** heat pumps, homes with damp or condensation problems, and anyone at home all day. In those cases, steady heating at a slightly lower temperature is usually the right call.
- **What it costs per hour:** 27p an hour to hold a warm house steady, up to about £1.08 an hour while the boiler is working flat out from cold.

The rest of this guide shows where those numbers come from, so you can see why the answer holds for most homes, where it stops holding, and what to do instead.

## Why leaving the heating on all day costs more

Your house is always leaking heat to the outside, through the walls, roof, windows, floor and every draught. The rate it leaks depends on one thing: the difference between the temperature inside and the temperature outside. A house at 20°C on a 5°C day is losing heat roughly a third faster than the same house at 16°C, because the gap is 15°C rather than 11°C.

The boiler has to replace every bit of heat that leaks out. So the total amount of gas you burn over a day depends on how warm the house was, on average, across the whole day. Every hour the house spends cooler is an hour it leaks less heat, and that saving is real even after you count the burst of gas needed to warm it back up.

That "warming back up" is the bit people get wrong. Reheating a cooled house does take a lot of energy at once, which is why the boiler runs hard and your smart meter display jumps when the heating comes on. But that energy only replaces the heat that leaked out while the house was cooling, and a cooling house leaks *less* than a warm one. Keeping the house warm all day means paying to replace a bigger leak for the whole time you are out.

A useful way to picture it: a bucket with a hole in the side. The fuller you keep the bucket, the faster the water runs out. Topping it up only when you need water wastes less than keeping it brimming all day.

## The numbers: what each heating pattern costs

To put real figures on this, we modelled a typical UK 3-bed semi-detached house on a cold winter day (5°C outside all day) with a gas boiler. The house loses about 200 watts for every degree of difference between inside and outside, which is a common figure for a semi with cavity wall and loft insulation. It takes around 30 hours for the house to cool most of the way to the outside temperature when the heating is off, which is typical of brick-built homes. The boiler can deliver up to 12kW of heat.

Gas costs **7.97p per kWh** under the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026. A modern condensing boiler is around 88-89% efficient, so each kWh of heat delivered to the radiators costs about **9p**. That is the same rounded figure we use in our [immersion heater](/blog/immersion-heater-running-costs-uk) and [boiler temperature](/blog/what-temperature-should-my-boiler-be-set-at) guides, so the comparisons across the site stay consistent.

| Heating pattern (5°C outside) | Heat used per day | Cost per day | vs 20°C all day |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20°C, 24 hours a day | 72 kWh | £6.48 | - |
| 18°C, 24 hours a day | 62 kWh | £5.61 | 13% cheaper |
| 20°C morning (6:30-8:30) and evening (4:30-10:30), off otherwise | 64 kWh | £5.75 | 11% cheaper |
| 20°C morning and evening, 17°C "low" the rest of the time | 65 kWh | £5.86 | 10% cheaper |
| 20°C evening only (5pm-10pm) | 54 kWh | £4.88 | 25% cheaper |

These are energy costs only. The gas standing charge of 29.68p a day is the same whatever pattern you choose, so it does not change the comparison.

Three things stand out.

**Timing the heating saves money, but less than many people expect.** In this house, heating only when you are home saves about 73p a day against leaving it on at 20°C, or roughly £110 across 150 cold days. That is worth having, but it is not the halving of the bill people sometimes imagine. The house does not go cold the moment the heating switches off. In our model it only drifts down to about 16°C overnight and during the working day.

**Turning the thermostat down is just as powerful.** Dropping from 20°C to 18°C, without changing the times at all, saves about 13%. If you are home all day, this is the lever that matters.

**A "low" background setting costs very little.** Setting the heating to hold 17°C when you are out, instead of switching it off, only adds about 11p a day in this house, because the house rarely cools below 17°C anyway in the time you are away. A setting of 15-16°C costs almost nothing extra, and that is often a sensible compromise if you want to avoid coming home to a cold house or are worried about damp.

### What changes in a draughty or well-insulated house

The size of the saving depends on how quickly your home loses heat.

| House type | Saving from timed heating vs 20°C all day | How cold it gets while off |
|---|---|---|
| Draughty or poorly insulated (cools quickly) | about 15%, 97p a day | about 15°C |
| Typical semi with cavity and loft insulation | about 11%, 73p a day | about 16°C |
| Well insulated, airtight (cools slowly) | about 7%, 49p a day | about 17.5°C |

So the leakier your home, the more you save by switching the heating off when you do not need it. That is the opposite of what the "leave it on low" advice assumes. In a well-insulated home the saving is smaller, but it is still a saving. In no case does leaving the heating on all day come out cheaper for a gas boiler.

If your home is in the draughty row, the bigger win is fixing the leak. Our [draught-proofing costs guide](/blog/draught-proofing-cost-uk) and [loft insulation guide](/blog/loft-insulation-cost-uk) show what each fix costs and how quickly it pays back.

## How much does it cost to have the heating on for 2 hours a day?

This is the other question people ask, and the honest answer is that it depends on how cold the house was before you started.

While the boiler is warming a cold house, it runs close to flat out. A typical combi or system boiler delivering 12kW of heat uses about 13.5kWh of gas an hour, which costs about **£1.08 an hour** at the current cap. So two hours of heating from cold costs roughly **£2.16** of gas.

Once the house is warm, the boiler only has to replace the heat leaking out. In our example semi on a 5°C day, that is about 3kW, or roughly **27p an hour**. A house that is already warm costs far less per hour to keep warm than one you are heating from scratch.

There is a catch with very short heating windows. In our model, two hours from a cold start is not enough for a typical semi to reach 20°C. The boiler runs flat out the whole time and the house is still warming up when it switches off. If you only want to run the heating for an hour or two, you will get more comfort for the money by:

- heating only the rooms you are using, by turning down the radiator valves in the others
- running the heating for the two hours just before and during the time you are in the room, not after you have sat down cold
- using a lower boiler flow temperature so the radiators feel warm for longer, which our [boiler temperature guide](/blog/what-temperature-should-my-boiler-be-set-at) explains

For one person in one room, a portable heater can sometimes beat the central heating over a short evening. Our [electric heater running costs guide](/blog/electric-heater-running-costs-uk) has the per-hour figures and shows where the crossover is.

## When leaving the heating on low does make sense

The "off when you are out" rule holds for most homes with a gas boiler. There are real exceptions, though, and they are the reason this myth refuses to die.

### You have a heat pump

Heat pumps are different. They are most efficient when they run steadily at a low flow temperature, and their efficiency drops when they have to work hard to recover a cold house. A heat pump forced to warm a house quickly often runs at a higher flow temperature and may switch on its backup electric heater, which can cost three times as much per unit of heat.

For this reason most installers recommend leaving a heat pump to run steadily, with at most a 1-2°C setback overnight. If you have one, "low and slow" is the right advice, and it does not contradict anything above. Our [heat pump running costs guide](/blog/heat-pump-running-costs-2026) and [heat pump cost guide](/heat-pump-cost-uk) cover how to set one up for the lowest bills.

### Your home has damp or condensation problems

A house that goes cold during the day can develop condensation on windows and cold walls, especially in older solid-walled homes. Holding a low background temperature, around 15-16°C, keeps surfaces above the point where moisture condenses. As the table above shows, that costs very little in most houses. If you are already seeing black mould or wet windowsills, our guide on [how to stop condensation on windows](/blog/how-to-stop-condensation-on-windows) covers the causes and fixes in more detail. A cheap [digital thermometer and humidity monitor](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=digital+thermometer+hygrometer+indoor&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) tells you whether a room is drifting into the danger zone, which is usually above 65% humidity.

### You are at home all day

If someone is in the house all day, there is no "off" period to save on, so the question becomes how warm to keep it. Here the thermostat setting is what matters. Each degree you turn it down saves roughly 6-7% of your heating gas in a typical house. Heating the rooms you are actually using, and turning down the radiators in spare rooms, saves more than any timing trick.

### Very cold snaps and frozen pipes

When temperatures fall well below freezing, a low frost setting protects pipes in lofts, garages and outside walls. Most boilers and thermostats have a frost-protection mode for this. It is cheap insurance, and it is very different from heating the whole house to 20°C while you are out.

## How to get the cheapest heating, in practice

1. **Use a timer or programmer.** Set the heating to come on about 30 minutes before you get up or get home, and to switch off 30 minutes before you leave or go to bed. The house holds its warmth for longer than you think.

2. **Turn the thermostat down 1°C.** Most people do not notice the difference between 20°C and 19°C, and it saves around 6-7% of your heating gas, which is worth roughly £40-£60 a year on a typical gas bill.

3. **Consider a smart thermostat if your schedule varies.** If you work shifts or come and go at odd times, a fixed timer will often heat an empty house. A smart thermostat such as [Hive](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=hive+smart+thermostat&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) or [tado](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=tado+wireless+smart+thermostat+starter+kit&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) can switch the heating off when everyone leaves and back on as you head home. Our guide to [whether smart thermostats save money](/blog/do-smart-thermostats-save-money-uk) covers which households gain the most.

4. **Heat rooms, not the whole house.** Thermostatic radiator valves let you keep the living room at 20°C and the spare bedroom at 15°C. [Smart radiator valves](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=smart+radiator+thermostat+valve+trv&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) go further and let you schedule each room separately.

5. **Lower the boiler flow temperature.** A condensing boiler is only fully efficient when the water returning to it is cool enough. Turning the flow temperature down to around 60°C helps it run in condensing mode for more of the time.

6. **Watch it on your smart meter.** Your in-home display shows gas use as it happens. Try a day with the heating timed and a day with it on low, and compare. That is the simplest way to settle the argument in your own house. If your display has stopped working, see our guide to the [best in-home displays and energy monitors](/blog/best-smart-meter-in-home-display-uk).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it cheaper to leave the heating on low all day?

Not with a gas boiler. A house kept warm all day loses heat faster than one that is allowed to cool while empty, so it uses more gas overall. In a typical 3-bed semi on a 5°C day, heating to 20°C only when you are home costs about £5.75, against about £6.48 for heating it all day. The exception is a heat pump, which works most efficiently when left running steadily.

### Does it cost more to heat a house from cold?

Heating a house from cold uses a lot of gas in a short time, which is why it feels expensive. But that gas only replaces the heat lost while the house was cooling, and a cooling house loses heat more slowly than a warm one. Over a full day, letting the house cool and then reheating it still uses less gas than keeping it warm the whole time.

### How much does it cost to run central heating for an hour?

About 27p an hour to keep a warm house warm, and up to about £1.08 an hour while the boiler is running flat out to heat a cold house, at the October to December 2026 gas price cap of 7.97p per kWh. The figures are for a typical 3-bed semi on a cold day. Bigger or draughtier homes cost more.

### How much does it cost to have the heating on for 2 hours a day?

Roughly £2 of gas a day if the house starts cold, because the boiler runs close to full power for the whole two hours. In practice, two hours from cold is often not long enough to warm a typical house fully, so time it for when you are actually in the room.

### Should I leave my heat pump on all the time?

Usually, yes. Heat pumps are most efficient when running steadily at a low flow temperature, and lose efficiency when they have to recover a cold house quickly. Most installers recommend leaving the heat pump on with at most a 1-2°C setback overnight.

### What temperature should I set my thermostat to in winter?

Most people are comfortable at 18-21°C in the rooms they use. Each degree lower saves roughly 6-7% of your heating gas. If anyone in the home is elderly, unwell or very young, keep the main living area at the warmer end of that range.

## The bottom line

For most UK homes with a gas boiler, leaving the heating on all day is not cheaper. A warm house leaks heat faster, so heating only when you need it uses less gas, and the leakier the house, the bigger the saving. Turning the thermostat down by a degree or two saves about as much as timing it carefully, and a low 15-16°C background setting costs very little if you want to avoid damp or a cold welcome home. The real exception is a heat pump, which should be left to run low and steady. Whatever system you have, your smart meter display will show you the answer for your own home within a couple of days.
