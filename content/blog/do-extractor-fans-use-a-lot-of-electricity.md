Do extractor fans use a lot of electricity? No. A typical bathroom extractor fan draws between 1W and 25W, so at the October 2026 price cap rate of **26.32p per kWh** it costs **well under 1p an hour** to run. Used for an hour a day, that is **£1-£2 a year**. Even a modern low-energy fan left running 24 hours a day costs less than a fiver a year.

**Quick answer (at 26.32p per kWh, October to December 2026):**

- **Older standard bathroom fan (about 20W):** 0.5p an hour, £1.92 a year at an hour a day.
- **Modern low-energy fan (under 8W):** 0.2p an hour, 77p a year at an hour a day.
- **Continuous "dMEV" fan (1.1W trickle, 4.1W boost):** about £3 a year running all day, every day.
- **Cooker hood (typically 100-250W on full):** 2.6p-6.6p an hour, so about £10 a year at 30 minutes a day on a 200W hood.
- **The cost people miss:** in winter the warm air a fan blows outside has to be reheated. For a fan on all day, that heat can cost ten times more than the electricity.

So the electricity is not the thing to worry about. The rest of this guide gives the numbers for every common type of fan, then deals with the part most articles skip: what the lost heat costs, and when it is worth paying to recover it.

## How much electricity does an extractor fan use?

An extractor fan's running cost is one sum: watts ÷ 1,000 × hours × the price of a kilowatt-hour. The wattage is printed on the fan's rating label or in its datasheet, and it is far lower than most people assume.

Older AC axial bathroom fans, the plain white square fans fitted in most UK bathrooms before about 2015, are commonly rated somewhere around 15-25W. Modern "low-energy" AC fans are usually under 10W. The Systemair BF Silent, for example, is rated **under 8W** on its [eFans product page](https://www.efans.co.uk/products/systemair-bf-silent-bathroom-extractor-fan-100mm-4?utm_source=thehomeenergyhub&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=do-extractor-fans-use-a-lot-of-electricity&utm_content=bf-silent). DC-motor continuous fans go lower still: the Greenwood Unity CV2GIP draws **1.1W on trickle and 4.1W on boost** according to its [specification](https://www.efans.co.uk/products/greenwood-cv2gip-dmev-continuous-extract-fan?utm_source=thehomeenergyhub&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=do-extractor-fans-use-a-lot-of-electricity&utm_content=cv2gip).

For comparison, a single old 40W incandescent bulb uses more than two older extractor fans, and a 2kW electric heater uses as much in one minute as a 20W fan uses in an hour and a half.

**To work it out for your own fan:** a 20W fan running for 3 hours uses 20 ÷ 1,000 × 3 = 0.06kWh, and 0.06 × 26.32p is about 1.6p. Change the wattage and hours and the same sum works for any fan.

The older standard fan is the only one in the table that gets anywhere near a noticeable figure, and only if it is left on around the clock. That usually happens by accident: a fan wired to the light in a cupboard WC, or a fan that never switches off because its timer has failed. If yours runs all day without being designed to, fix the timer or the wiring rather than the bill.

## Do bathroom extractor fans use a lot of electricity?

Bathroom fans are the smallest users of all, because they are small (100mm is standard) and they run for short bursts. A bathroom fan used for 20 minutes during and after each of two showers a day runs for about 40 minutes daily. On a 20W older fan that is **about £1.28 a year**. On an 8W modern fan it is **about 51p a year**.

Even if you leave the bathroom fan on overnight to clear a damp room, a 20W fan running for 8 hours costs about 4p. That is not where your electricity bill is going. If you are hunting for what is, our [running costs hub](/running-costs) covers every main appliance, and the [10-minute shower post](/blog/how-much-does-a-10-minute-shower-cost-uk) shows that the shower itself costs hundreds of times more than the fan above it.

## Intermittent vs continuous fans: which costs more?

**Intermittent fans** switch on with the light or a pull cord and usually run on for 15-20 minutes on a timer. They are off most of the day, so their electricity use is tiny.

**Continuous fans** (called dMEV, for decentralised mechanical extract ventilation) run all the time at a very low trickle speed, then boost when a humidity sensor or the light switch tells them to. Running all the time sounds expensive, but they are built for it. A DC-motor dMEV fan like the CV2GIP, trickling at 1.1W for 22 hours and boosting at 4.1W for two, uses about 12kWh a year, or **about £3**. That is less than an older intermittent fan used for two hours a day.

The catch with continuous fans is not electricity. It is heat, which the next section covers.

## The hidden cost: the warm air your fan blows outside

Every litre of air an extractor fan pushes outside is air you have paid to heat. Cold air leaks in to replace it, and your boiler or heat pump has to warm that up. Most of the pages ranking for this question skip the number, so here it is.

Heating air takes about 1.2kJ per cubic metre per degree. A bathroom fan extracting at 15 litres a second (the Building Regulations minimum for an intermittent bathroom fan) on a day when it is 20°C inside and 5°C outside removes heat at about 270W. For one hour that is 0.27kWh of heat. Replacing it with a gas boiler at the current cap (7.97p per kWh, or about 9p per kWh of delivered heat after boiler losses) costs **about 2.4p**. That is roughly **five times the electricity** a 20W fan uses in the same hour.

For a bathroom fan used in short bursts that still only adds up to a pound or two over a winter. It matters a lot more for a fan that runs continuously:

| Scenario | Heat lost over a heating season (200 days, 10°C average difference) | Cost to replace with gas at ~9p/kWh of heat |
|---|---|---|
| Intermittent fan, 15 l/s, 30 minutes a day | ~18kWh | ~£1.60 |
| Continuous dMEV fan on trickle, 8 l/s, all day | ~460kWh | ~£41 |
| Single-room heat recovery unit, 6 l/s trickle, 84% recovery | ~55kWh | ~£5 |

So a continuous fan that costs £3 a year in electricity can cost around £40 a year in heat, depending on how much air it moves and how cold the winter is. That is not an argument against continuous ventilation. In a well-sealed home the fan is doing a job that would otherwise be done by draughts, and a house with no ventilation gets damp and mouldy, which costs far more. But it does explain why heat recovery exists.

A single-room heat recovery unit passes the outgoing warm air over the incoming fresh air through a heat exchanger. The [Vent-Axia Lo-Carbon Tempra HTP](https://www.efans.co.uk/products/vent-axia-lo-carbon-tempra-htp-single-room-heat-recovery-unit-with-humidistat-timer-and-pullcord-443311?utm_source=thehomeenergyhub&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=do-extractor-fans-use-a-lot-of-electricity&utm_content=tempra-htp), for example, is rated at up to 84% heat recovery with a 6 l/s trickle speed, which is where the £5 figure above comes from. At around £446, against a saving of about £35 a year in heat compared with a continuous extract fan, it is a comfort and condensation purchase more than a money-saver. It pays back best in a cold, well-sealed bathroom where a fan has to run a lot. Whole-house heat recovery (MVHR) applies the same idea to every room; eFans' [MVHR running costs guide](https://www.efans.co.uk/blogs/help-advice/mvhr-running-costs?utm_source=thehomeenergyhub&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=do-extractor-fans-use-a-lot-of-electricity&utm_content=mvhr-running-costs) covers those numbers.

If you have recently [draught-proofed](/blog/draught-proofing-cost-uk) or [insulated your loft](/blog/loft-insulation-cost-uk), your house now leaks less air by accident. That makes deliberate ventilation more important, not less, and it is the point at which a humidity-controlled or continuous fan earns its place.

## Humidistat fans: the cheapest upgrade

A fan with a built-in humidity sensor switches itself on when the room gets damp and off when it clears. That fixes the two habits that waste the most: people switching a noisy fan off before the steam has gone, and fans running for an hour in an empty, dry room.

The electricity difference between a humidistat fan and a standard one is effectively nil. The BF Silent, for example, comes in Standard, Timer and Humidity and Timer versions at the same under-8W rating. The point of the humidistat is that it runs the fan for exactly as long as the room needs, which is the right amount for both the damp and the heat loss. If you want to see how damp your bathroom actually gets before choosing, a cheap [hygrometer humidity monitor](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=hygrometer+humidity+monitor&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) will tell you within a day or two. Readings that sit above about 70% for long after a shower point to a fan that is too weak or switching off too early.

## How much does a kitchen extractor or cooker hood cost to run?

Cooker hoods are the exception to "tiny". They move much more air than a bathroom fan and their motors are bigger, commonly in the region of 100-250W on full speed, plus a few watts for the lights. Check the rating plate inside the hood or the manual for your own figure.

At 200W, 30 minutes a day on full speed costs **about £9.61 a year**. Most people run a hood on a lower speed most of the time, which cuts that considerably. A recirculating hood (one with a charcoal filter and no duct to the outside) uses similar electricity but does not blow warm air outside, so it has no heat-loss cost. It also does not remove moisture, which is why a ducted hood or a separate kitchen extract fan is better for condensation.

## Is an extractor fan cheaper to run than a dehumidifier?

Much cheaper. A typical compressor dehumidifier draws about 200W, so it costs around 5p an hour, which is as much as running a modern bathroom fan for a day. Our [dehumidifier running costs guide](/blog/dehumidifier-running-costs-uk) has the full figures.

They do different jobs, though. An extractor fan removes damp air at its source, the shower or the hob, before it spreads. A dehumidifier dries air that has already spread through the house, which is what you need when drying washing indoors or tackling a damp bedroom. If you are fighting [condensation on windows](/blog/how-to-stop-condensation-on-windows), getting moisture out at the source with a fan that actually runs long enough is the cheapest fix, and a dehumidifier is the backup.

## Should you leave your extractor fan on?

For an **intermittent fan**: run it during your shower and for 15-20 minutes afterwards, with the bathroom door closed so it pulls the steam out rather than drawing warm air from the rest of the house. Leaving it on all day costs pennies in electricity but adds heat loss for no benefit once the room is dry.

For a **continuous dMEV fan**: yes, leave it on. That is how it is designed to work, and switching it off at the isolator to save £3 a year usually ends with mould on the ceiling.

For a fan that **runs constantly when it should not**, the cause is usually a failed overrun timer, a humidistat set too low, or a fan wired to a light that is left on. Fixing it saves a little electricity and more heat.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do extractor fans use a lot of electricity?

No. Most bathroom extractor fans draw between 1W and 25W, so they cost under 1p an hour at 26.32p per kWh. A modern low-energy fan left on all year uses about 70kWh, which is less than a typical fridge freezer uses in three months.

### How much does an extractor fan cost to run per hour?

Between about 0.03p and 0.7p an hour for a bathroom fan, depending on its wattage. A 20W older fan costs 0.53p an hour, an 8W modern fan 0.21p, and a 1.1W continuous fan on trickle 0.03p. Cooker hoods are higher, at roughly 2.6p-6.6p an hour on full speed.

### How much does a bathroom extractor fan cost to run per year?

Used for about an hour a day, £1-£2 a year for an older fan and under £1 for a modern low-energy one. A continuous dMEV fan running all day costs about £3 a year in electricity.

### Is it expensive to leave an extractor fan on all night?

No. A 20W fan running for 8 hours costs about 4p in electricity. In winter it also blows out some warm air, worth a few pence more in heating, so it is better to use a humidistat or timer than to leave it running by habit.

### Do continuous extractor fans cost a lot to run?

Not in electricity: a DC-motor dMEV fan costs about £3 a year. The larger cost is heat loss, which can be around £40 over a winter for a fan extracting 8 litres a second all day. Single-room heat recovery units cut that to about £5, but cost several hundred pounds to buy.

### Does an extractor fan make my house colder?

A little. It pulls in cold outside air to replace the warm air it removes. For a bathroom fan used in short bursts the effect is small. Closing the bathroom door while it runs, and using a humidistat so it stops when the room is dry, keeps it to a minimum.

## The bottom line

Extractor fans are among the cheapest things in your home to run, usually £1-£3 a year in electricity. Do not switch one off to save money: the damp it removes costs far more in mould, redecorating and heating a damp house than the fan ever will. If you want to cut the real cost, which is lost heat in winter, the order is a humidistat fan first, a low-energy continuous fan in a well-sealed home second, and heat recovery only where a fan has to run most of the day.
