So, how much does it cost to run a fridge freezer? At the Ofgem price cap rate of **26.32p per kWh**, a typical freestanding fridge freezer costs between about **£29 and £74 a year** to run, depending on its energy rating. That works out at roughly **8p to 20p a day**. A large American-style model costs nearer **£105 a year**, and a fridge freezer that is 15 or more years old can cost **£130 or more**.

Unlike a dishwasher or a kettle, a fridge freezer never switches off. It runs 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, so a small difference in efficiency adds up to a noticeable difference on your bill. This guide shows where the figures come from, what each energy rating and type costs, how to measure your own, and the simple changes that actually lower the cost.

All figures use 26.32p per kWh, the rate we use across our [appliance running cost guides](/running-costs). They are planning estimates for typical models rather than lab results, so check the kWh figure on your own appliance's energy label for an exact number.

## How to work out what your fridge freezer costs to run

Every appliance running cost comes from the same sum:

**Energy used (kWh) x your unit rate (p/kWh) = cost**

The difference with a fridge freezer is that you work it out per year rather than per use. Every fridge freezer sold in the UK carries an energy label, and the label shows its annual consumption in **kWh/annum**. That is the number you need. Multiply it by your unit rate and you have the yearly running cost.

For example, a fridge freezer rated at 220 kWh/annum costs 220 x 26.32p = **£57.90 a year**, or about 16p a day.

If you no longer have the label, search the model number online or look for the product information sheet, which lists the annual kWh for every model.

## How much does it cost to run a fridge freezer per year?

The energy label was rescaled in March 2021, so the best fridge freezers that used to be A+++ are now usually D or E, and most models on sale today are E or F rated. Here are typical figures for a standard 70/30 freestanding fridge freezer of around 300 litres:

| Energy rating (2021 label) | Typical energy use | Cost per year | Cost per day |
|---|---|---|---|
| A or B | 110 kWh | about £29 | about 8p |
| C or D | 160 kWh | about £42 | about 12p |
| E | 220 kWh | about £58 | about 16p |
| F | 280 kWh | about £74 | about 20p |
| Pre-2021, 10 to 15 years old | 400 kWh | about £105 | about 29p |
| 15+ years old or failing | 500 kWh | about £132 | about 36p |

The figures for older machines are broad, because their consumption varies with the condition of the door seals, the compressor and how often the freezer has been defrosted. But the pattern is clear: an old fridge freezer can easily cost three or four times as much to run as a modern A or B rated one.

## How much does it cost to run a fridge freezer per month?

Divide the yearly cost by 12. An A or B rated 70/30 fridge freezer costs about **£2.40 a month**, an E rated one about **£4.80** and an F rated one about **£6.15**. An American-style model costs about £8.75 a month, and a 15-year-old fridge freezer about £11. For most households, that makes it a £3 to £6 a month appliance: rarely the biggest item on the bill, but one of the steadiest, because it never stops.

## How much does it cost to run a fridge freezer per hour?

A fridge freezer does not run constantly at full power. The compressor switches on and off to hold the temperature, usually drawing somewhere around 80 to 150 watts while it is running and almost nothing when it is not. Over a typical day the compressor might run for a third to half of the time.

That is why the per-hour figure is tiny. An F rated model using 280 kWh a year averages about 0.03 kWh an hour, which is **under 1p an hour**. Even an old model using 500 kWh a year averages only about 1.5p an hour. The cost comes from the fact that those hours never stop.

## American-style, integrated and other types compared

Size and layout make as much difference as the energy rating. Here is how the main types compare at typical consumption:

| Type | Typical energy use | Cost per year |
|---|---|---|
| Under-counter fridge (no freezer) | 100 kWh | about £26 |
| Integrated 70/30 fridge freezer | 200 kWh | about £53 |
| Freestanding 70/30 fridge freezer, F rated | 280 kWh | about £74 |
| Upright freezer (separate) | 220 kWh | about £58 |
| Chest freezer | 180 kWh | about £47 |
| American-style side-by-side, E or F rated | 400 kWh | about £105 |
| American-style with ice and water dispenser | 450 kWh | about £118 |

A few things stand out:

- **American-style fridge freezers** cost roughly 40 to 60 per cent more to run than a standard model, mainly because they are much larger and the side-by-side doors let more cold air out. Ice and water dispensers add a little more.
- **Chest freezers** are the most efficient way to freeze food. Cold air sinks, so it stays in the cabinet when you lift the lid, and the insulation tends to be thick.
- **Integrated models** fitted behind a kitchen door are usually smaller and slightly cheaper to run than freestanding ones, though cramped ventilation in the housing can push their consumption up.

If you have a separate fridge and a separate freezer, add them together. Two appliances usually cost more to run than one combined fridge freezer of the same total capacity.

## What actually drives the cost

### The energy rating and age

This is the biggest single factor. A modern A or B rated fridge freezer uses about 110 kWh a year; an F rated one of the same size uses about 280 kWh; an old model can use 400 to 500 kWh. Compressors and insulation have improved a great deal, and door seals harden and leak as they age.

### Temperature settings

The colder you set it, the harder the compressor works. The Food Standards Agency recommends keeping your fridge at **5°C or below**, and freezers are designed to run at **-18°C**. Most fridges are set colder than they need to be. A [fridge and freezer thermometer](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=fridge+freezer+thermometer&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) costs a few pounds and tells you what the dial actually means. Aim for 3°C to 5°C in the fridge and -18°C in the freezer.

### Where it stands

A fridge freezer works by pumping heat out of the cabinet through the coils or panels at the back. If it stands next to an oven, a radiator or in direct sunlight, or if it is pushed hard against a wall with no air gap, it has to work harder to get rid of that heat. Leave the gap recommended in the manual, usually a few centimetres at the back and sides.

### Door seals and how often the door opens

A worn or split door seal lets warm air leak in all day. Test it with the paper test: close the door on a sheet of paper, and if it slides out easily, the seal is not doing its job. Replacement seals are available for most models. Leaving the door open while you decide what to cook has a smaller effect than people think, but it all adds up.

### Frost build-up

If you have a freezer that is not frost-free, ice on the walls acts as insulation in the wrong place and makes the compressor run for longer. Defrost it once the ice is more than a few millimetres thick. Frost-free models handle this automatically, using a small heater to melt the ice now and then, which is part of why they use slightly more energy.

### Dusty condenser coils

On older models, the black coils on the back collect dust and fluff, which stops them shedding heat properly. A [condenser coil cleaning brush](https://www.amazon.co.uk/s?k=fridge+condenser+coil+cleaning+brush&tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) and five minutes once or twice a year keeps them clear. Unplug the appliance first.

## How to measure what your own fridge freezer uses

The energy label figure is a lab test at a fixed room temperature. Your kitchen, your settings and the age of your appliance all move the real number, so it is worth measuring.

The easiest way is a plug-in energy monitor. A [Tapo P110 smart plug with energy monitoring](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0B831STBX?tag=thehomeenergyhub-21) records the kWh used and shows it in an app. Leave it in place for at least a week, because a fridge freezer's use rises and falls with how often it is opened and with defrost cycles, then multiply the weekly kWh by 52.

Two cautions. First, use the plug only to monitor, never to switch the fridge freezer off on a schedule, which would let the food warm up. Second, check that the plug is rated for the appliance; most domestic fridge freezers draw well within a 13A plug's limit, but follow the manufacturer's guidance. If your fridge freezer is integrated and the socket is hidden behind the units, a smart meter with an in-home display is the alternative. Switch other things off and watch the baseline load as the compressor cycles. Our [in-home display comparison](/blog/best-smart-meter-in-home-display-uk) explains how to read one.

## 9 ways to cut your fridge freezer running costs

1. **Check the temperature:** 3°C to 5°C in the fridge, -18°C in the freezer.
2. **Give it room to breathe**, away from the oven, radiators and direct sun.
3. **Clean the coils** on the back once or twice a year.
4. **Replace door seals** that fail the paper test.
5. **Defrost the freezer** if it is not frost-free and the ice is more than a few millimetres thick.
6. **Let hot food cool first**, then refrigerate it within a couple of hours, in line with food safety advice.
7. **Keep the freezer reasonably full.** Frozen food holds the cold; bags of bread or bottles of water fill gaps.
8. **Do not switch it off overnight.** It saves nothing, because the compressor works harder to pull the temperature back down.
9. **Measure before you replace.** An energy-monitoring plug gives you the real number to decide whether a new one is worth it.

## Is it worth replacing an old fridge freezer to save money?

Sometimes, but rarely on running costs alone for a fridge freezer under about ten years old.

Take an F rated model using 280 kWh a year. Replacing it with an A rated model using 110 kWh saves 170 kWh, which is about **£45 a year**. Top-rated fridge freezers often cost £700 to £1,000 or more, so that saving takes 15 years or more to pay back.

The sums are different for an old or failing appliance. If your fridge freezer is 15 or more years old and uses around 500 kWh a year, replacing it with a C rated model using 160 kWh saves 340 kWh, or about **£89 a year**. A £500 replacement pays for itself in under six years, and you get a fridge that keeps food properly cold.

Signs that an old fridge freezer is costing you more than it should include ice building up quickly in the freezer, a compressor that seems to run almost constantly, condensation inside the fridge, or a door seal that fails the paper test. If you see those, measure it for a week and do the sum.

## Does it matter where you keep a freezer, such as a garage?

Plenty of UK households keep a second freezer in the garage. That can work well, but check the **climate class** on the label or in the manual. Classes such as SN (sub-normal) and N are designed for rooms down to 10°C or 16°C; a model rated for a cold room is needed if the garage drops close to freezing in winter. Some fridge freezers with a single thermostat in the fridge section can stop running the freezer properly when the surrounding air is cold, because the fridge thinks it is cold enough already.

Summer heat has the opposite effect. A freezer in a hot garage or conservatory works much harder in July than in January, so its yearly use can be well above the label figure.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to run a fridge freezer per day?

About 8p to 20p a day for a typical modern 70/30 fridge freezer at 26.32p per kWh, depending on its energy rating. An American-style model costs around 29p a day, and a fridge freezer that is 15 or more years old can cost 36p a day or more.

### How much does it cost to run a fridge freezer for a year?

About £29 a year for an A or B rated model, £58 for an E rated one and £74 for an F rated one. American-style models cost around £105 to £118 a year. Check the kWh/annum figure on your energy label and multiply it by your unit rate for an exact figure.

### How much electricity does a fridge freezer use?

A modern freestanding fridge freezer uses roughly 110 to 280 kWh a year, depending on its energy rating and size. An American-style fridge freezer uses around 400 kWh a year, and old models can use 500 kWh or more. While the compressor is running it draws roughly 80 to 150 watts.

### Is it cheaper to run a fridge freezer or a separate fridge and freezer?

Usually a single fridge freezer. Two separate appliances have two compressors, more outer surface losing cold and two sets of door seals. A chest freezer is the exception: it is efficient enough that a fridge plus a chest freezer can cost about the same as one large fridge freezer.

### Should I turn my fridge freezer off at night to save money?

No. It saves almost nothing, because the compressor has to work harder to bring the temperature back down in the morning, and it lets food sit at unsafe temperatures for hours.

## The bottom line

A typical fridge freezer costs about **£29 to £74 a year** to run, or roughly **8p to 20p a day**. American-style and older models cost more, at £105 to £130 or more a year. The cheapest wins are the free ones: set the right temperature, give it space, keep the seals and coils in good shape, and defrost the freezer when needed.

If you think yours is costing more than it should, measure it with an energy-monitoring plug for a week before you buy a new one. That one number tells you whether replacing it will pay for itself, or whether a few minutes of maintenance is all it needs.
